Football belongs
to everyone.

A proposal for a more inclusive, player-conscious and unpredictable future for the world's game.

Our starting point

Football is the world's game. It brings together people from different cultures, languages and backgrounds. A ball, a pitch and a dream can connect millions of people.

Yet we should keep asking whether the sport is organised in the best possible way for players, supporters and countries at every level. Football should be about competition, passion, unforgettable moments and the possibility that anything can happen—not only about protecting established powers or preserving systems because they already exist.

This manifesto sets out proposals for a football world where every willing nation has an opportunity to participate, players have time to recover, decisions are more reliable and the game remains unpredictable.

1. A better football calendar

Proposal: club football should run from February to November.

December and January should be reserved for rest, recovery, family time and preparation for the next season. Players are human beings, not machines. They deserve a meaningful opportunity to spend Christmas and New Year with their families without competitive fixtures dominating the period.

January could be used for pre-season training, fitness preparation and warm-weather training camps. Clubs would have a defined period to prepare before the new campaign begins.

Make summer matchdays better

Where local weather and conditions allow, matches should be scheduled for late afternoons and evenings rather than the hottest hours of the day. Warm evenings, a setting sun, packed stadiums and supporters enjoying themselves can make matchday special. Kick-off times must still protect players and fans from extreme heat and respect local conditions.

A season ending in November could also create the possibility of celebrating league titles and European trophies before Christmas. The calendar would need careful international coordination and practical transition planning.

2. A World Cup for the whole world

Proposal: every FIFA member association should be invited to participate. No qualifying campaign should decide who is allowed to dream.

The World Cup should represent the world. Every FIFA member association would receive an invitation, regardless of population, wealth, footballing reputation or current level of ability. Participation would remain voluntary: countries that do not wish to enter would not be forced to do so.

At least three matches for every participant

Every participating country would play at least three group-stage matches. These games would provide international experience and give supporters multiple opportunities to see their national team compete on the world's biggest stage.

Plan it over four years

A tournament on this scale would require a four-year planning cycle covering the competition format, host arrangements, venues, transport, accommodation, security, player recovery and tournament operations. The format would need to be designed around the actual number of participating countries and the capacity available.

The ambition is enormous. That is a reason to examine the logistics honestly and plan carefully—not to dismiss the principle before assessing what could be possible.

3. One draw. No special treatment.

Proposal: the World Cup draw should be completely random.

No seeded pots. No protected favourites. No geographical restrictions. No artificial balancing of groups or knockout paths.

A group could contain France, England, Germany and Spain. Another could contain four countries that have never reached the later stages of a major tournament. The draw would not try to protect famous teams or guarantee that the biggest nations progress.

If San Marino receives a favourable route, wins its group and reaches the final, then it has earned the opportunity to compete for the trophy. The strongest teams would still have their quality, preparation and experience, but they would not receive a protected route simply because they are famous or commercially valuable.

Let the draw decide the path. Let the football decide the winner.

A World Cup should make room for uncertainty, underdog stories, unexpected heroes and moments nobody could have predicted.

4. Support for smaller football nations

Footballing opportunity should not depend entirely on national wealth. A country may have talented players, passionate supporters and a genuine desire to participate but lack the resources to cover the costs.

A dedicated participation fund should help eligible nations cover essential tournament expenses such as travel, accommodation, preparation and other reasonable costs. It should have transparent criteria, independent auditing and safeguards against misuse.

Financial support would not guarantee sporting success. It would help ensure that financial hardship does not automatically prevent a willing country from taking part.

5. Refereeing technology that serves football

Refereeing mistakes can decide championships, eliminate teams and leave players and supporters feeling that their efforts have been wasted. Technology should be used effectively to reduce avoidable errors.

Goal-line technology, automated offside assistance and video review can contribute when implemented appropriately. Decisions and reviews should be transparent, efficient and understandable to supporters.

Human referees would remain responsible for managing matches, interpreting situations that require judgement and maintaining player safety. Technology should support them, not create more confusion or unnecessary interruptions.

The objective is simple: fewer preventable mistakes, more consistent decisions and greater confidence in the fairness of competition.

6. Clear continental eligibility rules

Proposal: continental competitions should use clear, consistently applied geographic criteria.

For European national-team and club competitions, the proposed rule would be based on the proportion of a country's territory located in Europe:

  • More than 50% of territory in Europe: eligible for European competitions under the proposed rule.
  • Exactly 50%: the country could choose between the European and Asian systems.
  • Less than 50%: the country would compete in the appropriate non-European system.

Under this proposed rule, Russia and Turkey would not qualify for European competitions on geographic grounds, while Israel would be classified outside Europe.

This is a proposed reform, not a description of current UEFA eligibility rules. Implementation would require agreed definitions of continental boundaries, a process for disputed cases, and transition arrangements for clubs and national teams.

7. International football without constant disruption

Representing one's country is one of the greatest honours in football. International football should remain central to the sport, but the calendar can place repeated demands on players and disrupt domestic seasons.

International football should be consolidated into clearer windows wherever possible, reducing repeated interruptions while preserving meaningful competition, preparation time and opportunities for national teams to develop.

This would require cooperation between FIFA, continental confederations, domestic leagues, clubs and player representatives. The goal is not to diminish international football, but to organise it in a way that respects both the national team and the club game.

8. Put people before commercial convenience

Football generates enormous revenue and attracts a global audience. Financial stability matters, and competitions need sustainable plans. But money should not be the only measure of success.

A tournament that creates opportunities, produces unforgettable stories and gives supporters new reasons to love football has value beyond its commercial return. Decisions should consider players, supporters, smaller associations, grassroots football and future generations—not only the biggest clubs and wealthiest markets.

Football's global popularity creates both an opportunity and a responsibility: protect the integrity of competition while expanding access to it.

Our principles

OpportunityEvery willing nation should have a meaningful chance to participate.
Equal treatmentNo country should receive preferential treatment because of reputation.
UnpredictabilityRandom draws should allow underdogs to dream.
Player welfareCalendars should provide adequate rest and recovery.
IntegrityTechnology should reduce preventable errors.
TransparencyRules should be clear, consistent and publicly understood.
AccessibilityFinancial barriers should not automatically exclude willing nations.
PassionFootball should remain a source of joy, identity and shared experiences.

The future we want

Imagine a World Cup where every willing nation has a place. Imagine a random draw that sends favourites into difficult groups and gives an underdog a route to the final. Imagine a football calendar that respects recovery and gives families their holidays back.

These ideas require serious planning, cooperation and reform. Some would be more difficult to implement than others. They should be debated, tested and improved rather than dismissed simply because they challenge the current system.

Football is not just for the biggest countries, the richest clubs or the most famous players.

Football belongs to everyone.
